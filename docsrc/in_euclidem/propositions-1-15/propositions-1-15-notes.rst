Commentary on Propositions I.1–I.15: Triangles, Congruence & Perpendiculars — GEOMETOR Commentary & Concordance Notes
=====================================================================================================================

GEOMETOR Philosophical & Mathematical Commentary
------------------------------------------------

1. **Zeno of Sidon's Continuity & Uniqueness Critiques (`Friedlein 214–218`)**:
   In :just:`Prop. I.1 <I.1>`, two questions arise that modern axiomatics (Pasch, Hilbert) later formalized: (a) why must the two circles intersect? and (b) as :person:`Zeno of Sidon <zeno-of-sidon>` asked, how do we know the two radii $CA, CB$ do not share a common segment near $C$? In `geometor/model`, exact polynomial intersection of the two unit circles $(x)^2 + y^2 = 1$ and $(x - 1)^2 + y^2 = 1$ algebraically exhibits the two conjugate equilateral vertices $(1/2, \pm \sqrt{3}/2)$ over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})$, while Zeno's uniqueness critique highlights the implicit uniqueness content of :just:`Postulates 1–2 <I.post.1>` defended by :person:`Posidonius <posidonius>`.
2. **Pappus's Zero-Construction Symmetry Proof of `I.5` (`Friedlein 249–250`)**:
   Preserved exclusively here (`proclus:in-euclid:249.20-250.12`), :person:`Pappus's <pappus>` proof of :just:`Prop. I.5 <I.5>` conceives the single isosceles triangle $ABC$ ($AB = AC$) simultaneously as $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle ACB$, applying :just:`Prop. I.4 <I.4>` directly without a single auxiliary line—an early triumph of transformational/reflection symmetry in synthetic geometry.
3. **Trisection, Indivisible Lines & Oenopides' Gnomon (`Friedlein 272–289`)**:
   Proclus's distinction between straightedge-and-compass angle bisection (:just:`I.9 <I.9>`) and general angle trisection (`Friedlein 272`, requiring cubic/transcendental curves—conics, conchoids, or quadratrices) anticipates Wantzel's 1837 algebraic degree criterion ($2^k$ vs. degree $3$), while :person:`Oenopides' <oenopides>` astronomical perpendicular (:just:`I.12 <I.12>`, `Friedlein 283, 7–10`) anchors Book I's constructive restrictions in classical celestial measurement.
