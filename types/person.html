Types / person

Content of type 'person'

Amphinomus

Joined Speusippus on theorems vs. problems and collaborated with Menaechmus on the logic of convertible theorems Friedlein loci: 77, 181, 200, 202, 254.

Amyclas

Associate of Plato who collaborated in the Academy to perfect geometric science Friedlein loci: 67.

Anaxagoras

Touched upon many questions belonging to geometry after Pythagoras, according to the Eudemian Summary Friedlein loci: 65.

Apollonius

Transferred Pythagorean application-of-areas terms (parabola, hyperbola, ellipse) to conic sections; proposed alternative proofs for Common Notion 1 and Props. I.10, I.11, I.23 Friedlein loci: 71, 100, 183, 194, 279, 282, 335, 419.

Archimedes

Synchronism with Eratosthenes noted at p. 68; cited for shortest-path straight line definition, spirals, Equilibria of Planes, and crown hydrostatics Friedlein loci: 63, 68, 104, 110, 181, 272.

Archytas

Increased the number of theorems and brought them into a more scientific arrangement alongside Leodamas and Theaetetus Friedlein loci: 66.

Aristotle

Formulated the epistemology of axioms, postulates, and hypotheses, commensurately universal demonstration, and ‘intelligible matter’; critiqued by Proclus on abstraction Friedlein loci: 12–16, 48–53, 64, 75–76, 139, 178–183, 371, 381.

Athenaeus

Lived in the Academy with Eudoxus’s successors and became eminent in geometry and the other mathematical sciences Friedlein loci: 67.

Barocius

Published the 1560 Padua Latin edition from Cretan and Venetian manuscripts, healing major corruptions and lacunae of the 1533 Basel Greek editio princeps Friedlein loci: ed. 1560.

Carpus

Defined angle as distance/divergence between lines and argued in his Astronomical Treatise that problems precede theorems in natural order Friedlein loci: 125–126, 241–243.

Dinostratus

Applied the quadratrix to the squaring of the circle and perfected geometry in the Academy Friedlein loci: 67.

Eratosthenes

Cited as contemporary of Archimedes and critic of the nature of proportion as the bond of mathematics Friedlein loci: 7, 43, 68.

Euclid

Synthesized the Elements under Ptolemy I, bringing to irrefutable demonstration what predecessors had proved loosely; revered by Proclus as Platonic by philosophical conviction Friedlein loci: 68–84, 208–432.

Eudemus

Pupil of Aristotle whose lost History of Geometry is Proclus’s primary source for pre-Euclidean geometry, Thales, Oenopides, the Pythagorean angle-sum proof, and application of areas Friedlein loci: 64–70, 125, 157, 250, 299, 333, 352, 379, 419.

Eudoxus

Associate of Plato’s circle; first increased the number of general theorems, added three proportions, and extended Plato’s inquiries on ‘the section’ using analysis Friedlein loci: 67.

Friedlein

Edited the standard 1873 Teubner critical Greek edition from Munich, Vatican, Bologna, and Oxford manuscripts Friedlein loci: ed. 1873.

Geminus

Proclus’s richest Hellenistic source on classification of sciences, curves, postulates vs. axioms, uniform flux (quoted in Greek by Hoenen), and asymptotic cautions on Postulate 5 Friedlein loci: 38–42, 105, 111, 126, 176–192, 241, 251.

Heath

Author of the monumental three-volume commentary in geometor/euclid, citing Friedlein’s Proclus at more than 150 loci Friedlein loci: Heath I–III.

Pseudocode · Heath

Hermotimus

Pursued the investigations begun by Eudoxus and Theaetetus, discovered many elements, and composed treatises on Loci Friedlein loci: 67.

Heron

Reduced authentic Common Notions to three and supplied direct and construction-sparing proofs for Props. I.19, I.20, I.24, I.25, and I.47 Friedlein loci: 41, 196, 305, 323, 340, 346, 429.

Hippias

Invented the quadratrix (tetragonizousa) curve used for trisecting rectilinear angles and squaring the circle Friedlein loci: 272, 356.

Hippocrates Of Chios

Discovered the quadrature of lunes, pioneered geometric reduction (apagoge, reducing cube duplication to two mean proportionals), and was the first recorded composer of Elements Friedlein loci: 66, 213.

Hoenen

Professor at the Pontifical Gregorian University (geometor/hoenen), engaging Proclus on sensible inexactitude, phantasia, intelligible matter, and Postulate 5 Friedlein loci: Hoenen 1954.

Iamblichus

Inspired the Athenian Academy’s synthesis of Pythagorean-Platonic mathematics in his On Pythagoreanism / De Communi Mathematica Scientia Friedlein loci: 20–47.

Leodamas

Contemporary of Archytas and Theaetetus; received the method of geometric analysis from Plato, discovering many things in geometry Friedlein loci: 66, 211.

Leon

Pupil of Neoclides who composed more careful Elements and discovered diorismoi determining when a sought problem is possible or impossible Friedlein loci: 66–67.

Mamercus

Brother of the poet Stesichorus; remembered in the Eudemian Summary for acquiring great fame through his zeal for geometry Friedlein loci: 65.

Marinus

Succeeded Proclus at Athens, wrote Vita Procli, and authored the Preface to Euclid’s Data Friedlein loci: passim.

Menaechmus

Analyzed the dual meaning of ‘element’ (stoicheion), defended ‘problems’ alongside ‘theorems’, and developed the theory of geometric conversion Friedlein loci: 67, 72–73, 78, 181, 254.

Menelaus

Author of the Sphaerica; contributed a concise direct demonstration of Prop. I.25 (converse Hinge Theorem) preserved by Proclus Friedlein loci: 345–346.

Neoclides

Younger than Leodamas and teacher of Leon; added many discoveries to those of his predecessors Friedlein loci: 66.

Nicomedes

Invented the conchoidal lines (konchoeideis), cited by Geminus and Proclus both for angle trisection and as asymptotic curves that approach a line forever without meeting Friedlein loci: 111, 177, 272.

Oenopides

Slightly younger than Anaxagoras; first investigated dropping a perpendicular (I.12, calling it kata gnomona) and copying a rectilinear angle (I.23), and distinguished theorems from problems Friedlein loci: 65–66, 80, 283, 333.

Pappus

Author of the celebrated zero-construction symmetry proof of Prop. I.5 (pons asinorum) and critical remarks on Postulate 4 converses and additional axioms Friedlein loci: 189, 197, 249–250, 429.

Perseus

Investigated the three plane sections of the torus (speira), commemorated in an epigram preserved by Proclus Friedlein loci: 111–112.

Philippus

Carried out geometric investigations according to Plato’s instructions; marks the endpoint of Eudemus’s chronicle Friedlein loci: 67–68, 307.

Philo

Devised a celebrated direct three-case proof of SSS triangle congruence (Prop. I.8) bypassing Euclid’s Prop. I.7 Friedlein loci: 266–268.

Plato

Catalyzed the greatest advance in mathematics and geometry; formulated the Divided Line, recollection, analytical method (transmitted to Leodamas), optical straight-line definition, and even-number right-triangle rule Friedlein loci: 3–46, 66, 109, 211, 428.

Plutarch Of Athens

Teacher of Syrianus and aged mentor of young Proclus; cited with reverence on the multi-categorical nature of the angle Friedlein loci: 125.

Porphyry

Furnished incisive geometric analyses, case completions, and direct proofs in Book I (Props. I.14, I.18, I.20, I.24, I.26) Friedlein loci: 56, 255, 297, 315, 323–326, 347–352.

Posidonius

Wrote a dedicated treatise refuting Zeno of Sidon; proposed enclosing-limit definition of figure, equidistant definition of parallels, and sevenfold classification of quadrilaterals Friedlein loci: 80, 143, 170, 176, 200, 214–218.

Proclus

Head of the Athenian Academy for nearly fifty years; composed the Commentary on Book I of Euclid’s Elements uniting Neoplatonic epistemology of phantasia, Eudemian history, and rigorous axiomatic analysis Friedlein loci: 1–436.

Protagoras

Argued against geometers that a physical circle touches a straightedge along a line rather than at a single point; refuted by Proclus and cited by Hoenen Friedlein loci: 139–140.

Ptolemy

Composed a treatise attempting to prove Euclid’s Fifth Postulate; Proclus preserves Ptolemy’s lemmas and attempted proof of I.29 while diagnosing its circularity Friedlein loci: 191, 362–368.

Pythagoras

Transformed geometry into a liberal philosophical discipline, investigating first principles intellectually and discovering cosmic figures and proportionals; credited by tradition with I.47 Friedlein loci: 35, 65, 426–428.

Speusippus

Maintained against Menaechmus that all geometric propositions should be called ‘theorems’ rather than ‘problems’ because eternal objects have no temporal generation Friedlein loci: 77–78, 179, 181, 200.

Syrianus

Expositor of ontological projection in phantasia and the horn-angle problem, whose lectures inspired Proclus’s Commentary Friedlein loci: 123, 125, 432.

Taylor

Published the two-volume English translation (1788–1789; reissued 1792) defending Platonic mathematics against modern empiricism Friedlein loci: ed. 1788–1789.

Thales

First brought geometry from Egypt to Hellas; credited via Eudemus with bisecting the circle by its diameter (Def. 17), equality of isosceles base angles (I.5), equality of vertical angles (I.15), and ASA congruence for ship distances at sea (I.26) Friedlein loci: 65, 157, 250, 299, 352.

Theaetetus

Co-architect of Book X irrationals and Book XIII cosmic polyhedra; honored in the Eudemian Summary Friedlein loci: 66–67.

Theodorus

Eminent in geometry alongside Hippocrates of Chios; famed for proving the irrationality of non-square surds up to 17 Friedlein loci: 66.

Theudius

Arranged the Elements admirably and generalized many partial propositions Friedlein loci: 67.

Xenocrates

Advocated indivisible lines (atomoi grammai), whose geometric impossibility Proclus demonstrates from Prop. I.10 (bisection of every finite segment) Friedlein loci: 277–279.

Zeno Of Sidon

Accepted Euclid’s principles solely to argue that Prop. I.1 still fails unless one also postulates that two straight lines (or circles) cannot share a common segment Friedlein loci: 199–200, 214–218.
