Prologue Part II: Philosophy & History of Geometry — Analytical Summary¶

Scope: Friedlein pp. 48–84 · Book II (Chaps. I–IX)

Architectural Argument of Prologue Part II¶

Turning from universal mathematics to geometry proper, Prologue Part II (Friedlein pp. 48–84; Taylor Book II) resolves the central epistemological paradox of geometry, preserves the foundational history of Greek mathematics, and defines the deductive purpose of Euclid's Elements:

  1. Mathematical Imagination (*Phantasia*) & *Probole* (*Friedlein* 48–63): How can geometry speak of two equal circles, bisect a line, or superpose one triangle upon another if the pure concept (logos) of the circle or triangle in dianoia is unique, indivisible, and unextended? Proclus answers with his celebrated doctrine of projection: dianoia projects its partless internal logoi outward onto the luminous screen of mathematical imagination, which serves as intelligible matter (ὕλη νοητή). Like a thinker beholding his own reflection in a clear mirror, the geometer constructs, divides, and compares extended yet sensible-free figures in phantasia while reasoning universally from the logos within dianoia.

  2. The Eudemian Summary of Greek Geometry (*Friedlein* 64–70): Excerpting Eudemus of Rhodes's lost History of Geometry, Proclus traces the ascent of geometry from sensible measurement along the Nile to pure science: - Thales of Miletus brings geometry from Egypt to Greece; Mamercus wins renown; Pythagoras elevates geometry into liberal philosophy, discovering proportionals/irrationals and the cosmic figures. - Anaxagoras and Oenopides of Chios advance astronomical geometry; Hippocrates of Chios squares lunes and composes the first Elements, alongside Theodorus of Cyrene. - Plato inspires an explosion of discovery in the Academy: Leodamas of Thasos, Archytas of Tarentum, and Theaetetus; Neoclides and Leon (inventor of diorismoi); Eudoxus of Cnidus (general theorems, proportions, and the section via analysis); Amyclas, Menaechmus, Dinostratus, Theudius of Magnesia, Athenaeus, Hermotimus, and Philippus of Medma. - Finally, Euclid—living under Ptolemy I (”there is no royal road to geometry”)—systematizes the Elements, perfecting Eudoxus’s theorems, Theaetetus’s irrationals, and bringing earlier loose proofs to irrefragable demonstration.

  3. Purpose (*Skopos*) & *Stoicheiosis* (*Friedlein* 70–84): Proclus distinguishes the cosmic skopos of the whole thirteen-book Elements (constructing the five Platonic solids of the Timaeus) from its pedagogical skopos as an elementary system training the intellect in rigorous theorems and problems. Note also how Prologue II’s discussion of first principles and propositions (Friedlein 75–81) prepares directly for Proclus’s formal analysis of the Six Canonical Parts of a Geometric Proposition at Friedlein 203–207 (Problems vs. Theorems & The Six Canonical Parts of a Proposition).
