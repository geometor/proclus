Hypotheses, Postulates, Axioms & Propositional Form — Analytical Summary¶

Scope: Friedlein pp. 178–207 · Book III (Petitions, Axioms & Prop. I Prefatory Sections)

Architectural Argument of Postulates, Axioms & Propositional Form¶

Bridging Book I’s Definitions and its forty-eight Propositions, Friedlein pp. 178–207 (Taylor Book III opening) establishes both the epistemology of unproved geometric principles and the formal logic of Euclidean demonstration:

  1. Hypotheses, Postulates, and Axioms (*Friedlein* 178–184): Reviewing three classical schools (Speusippus/Amphinomus, Geminus, and Aristotle in Posterior Analytics I.10), Proclus distinguishes axioms (self-evident cognitive truths common across sciences) from postulates (constructive geometric demands easily granted without complex machinery).

  2. Straightedge, Compass, and Right Angles (*Friedlein* 185–191): - Postulates 1–3 ground the entire constructive universe of straightedge and compass in the uniform, undeviating flux of a point (quoting Geminus at Friedlein 185) and the revolution of a bounded distance around a stationary center. - Postulate 4 (all right angles are equal) reflects the determinacy of perpendicularity; Proclus also records Pappus's acute observation (Friedlein 189–190) that the converse fails if curvilinear lunular angles are admitted.

  3. Critique of the Fifth Postulate (*Friedlein* 191–193): In one of the most consequential passages in mathematical history, Proclus declares that Postulate 5 ought to be struck completely from the postulates because it lacks self-evidence, its converse (Prop. I.17) is a proved theorem, and asymptotic curves (such as the hyperbola and conchoid) prove that continuous convergence alone does not entail intersection! He defers his full examination of Ptolemy's and his own attempted proofs to Prop. I.29 (Prop. I.29: Ptolemy’s Treatise & Proclus’s Attempted Proof of Postulate 5).

  4. Critique of the Common Notions (*Friedlein* 193–198): Proclus refutes Apollonius's circular attempt to prove Common Notion 1, rejects scholiastic axiom inflation (such as “two straight lines do not enclose a space”), and compares Heron's three-axiom canon with Euclid’s five.

  5. Problems vs. Theorems & The Six Canonical Parts (*Friedlein* 199–207): Mediating the Academy debate between Speusippus and Menaechmus, Proclus codifies the six canonical parts of a complete Euclidean proposition (Friedlein 203–207): - Πρότασις (Enunciation): states universally what is given and what is sought. - Ἔκθεσις (Setting-out): instantiates the datum in a concrete lettered figure. - Διορισμός (Specification): isolates the sought conclusion on that particular figure. - Κατασκευή (Construction): adds the auxiliary lines and circles required for proof. - Ἀπόδειξις (Demonstration): deduces the target scientifically from causes and prior principles. - Συμπέρασμα (Conclusion): ascends back from the lettered instance to the universal enunciation, sealed by ὅπερ ἔδει ποιῆσαι (Q.E.F.) or ὅπερ ἔδει δεῖξαι (Q.E.D.).
